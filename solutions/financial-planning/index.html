Do you know what your money needs to do, and when?

Why Few Indians Save for Old Age

  1. Most Set Goals; Fewer Know How to Reach Them

    In NCFE's national survey, 82% of adults said they set long-term financial goals and strive to achieve them, and 74% of households kept a budget. But 49% met the survey's test of financial knowledge, and 27% met all three tests of financial literacy (knowledge, behaviour and attitude).[2]

  2. Where the Monthly Budget Goes

    Across households, 44% of monthly spending goes on essentials such as rent, groceries and bills, and 23% on other spending such as eating out and travel. 15% goes into savings, 9% into investments and 9% into loan repayments.[3]

  3. Insurance Cover Is About Half the World's

    Insurance premiums came to 3.7% of India's GDP in 2024-25, 2.7% for life insurance and 1.0% for other insurance, which includes health. Across the world, premiums came to 7.3% of GDP in 2024.[4]

  4. Where People Turn for Money Information

    Among investors, 59% gather information from friends, family and colleagues, and 56% from financial influencers on social media. 25% turn to financial professionals such as advisers, chartered accountants and bank staff.[3]

Goals are many, and retirement comes last among them. Asked for their main goal in investing, 20% of households named children's education and 16% supporting their family; 4% named retirement.[3]

Financial Planning Steps You Can Take Yourself

  1. Know where the money goes. Write down a month's income and spending: essentials, other spending, EMIs, saving and investing. The order of the steps below matters more than exact figures.
  2. Build an emergency fund first. Of Indian adults, 60.7% could keep paying their expenses, by any means, for a month or less if their main income stopped.[1] Six months of essential spending is a common choice; the Emergency Fund page works out yours.
  3. Cover the people who depend on you. Life cover for each earner with dependants, and health cover for the family, pay out when savings cannot. Insurance is protection, not an investment for your goals.
  4. Clear costly debt. Credit card balances and personal loans charge high interest, and paying them off saves that interest for certain. The EMI Management page works out what prepaying saves.
  5. Put a cost and a date on each goal. At the calculator's numbers, your goals cost ₹21,00,000 today and ₹52,42,040 by their dates, and need ₹12,353 a month in all at the rate you chose: an illustration, not a promise of any return. Households put about 9% of their spending into investments, ₹3,600 a month on a ₹40,000 budget.[3]
  6. Work out retirement on its own. It needs a sum that lasts for decades after your income stops. The Retirement page works it out.
  7. Match each goal's money to its time. Money for a goal a year or two away does not belong in equity; money for a goal 15 years away has time to ride out falls. The table below marks the row for each goal.
  8. Review once a year. Incomes, costs and goals change. Check each goal every year, and bring your mix back to its target: the Asset Allocation page shows how.
  9. Get a full plan if you need one. A SEBI-registered investment adviser can prepare one for your situation.

Financial Planning Questions People Ask

What are the steps of financial planning?

Most guides give a similar order: know your income and spending, build an emergency fund, insure the people who depend on you, clear costly debt, put a cost and a date on each goal, invest for each goal by its time, and review once a year. The steps above follow it; SEBI's guide under Read and Share sets out its own.

Can I do financial planning on my own?

Many people can, with a budget, a list of goals and a calculator like the one above. The official guides under Read and Share explain each step. For a full plan for your own situation, a SEBI-registered investment adviser can prepare one.

What is the 50-30-20 rule?

A rule of thumb: 50% of take-home pay for needs, 30% for wants and 20% for saving and investing. It is not an official rule. Across Indian households, saving and investing together came to 24% of monthly spending in SEBI's survey.[3]

How much should I save from my salary?

Enough to pay for your goals, which the calculator above works out: ₹12,353 a month at its numbers. Households that invest in market products put 17% of their monthly income into investments.[3] Raising the amount each year as your pay rises makes a large goal easier to reach.

What is the difference between saving and investing?

Saving sets money aside, usually in a bank account or deposit, where it is easy to reach and changes little in value. Investing puts it into assets such as equity or bonds, whose value can rise or fall, so that it can grow faster than prices over the years. In 2024, 38.6% of Indian adults saved any money in the past year, and 27.0% saved at a bank or through a mobile money account.[1]
