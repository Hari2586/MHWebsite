Source: AMFI and Crisil, AMFI Factbook 2026, p.36.[1]

Why Fewer Women Invest in India

  1. About 1 in 4 Mutual Fund Investors Is a Woman

    Women held 34.5% of individual investors' mutual fund assets in March 2026, ₹15,88,000 Crores, up from 32.6% in March 2021.[1]

  2. Fewer Women Are in Paid Work

    In 2025, 40.0% of women aged 15 and over were working or looking for work, against 79.1% of men. Among women outside the labour force, 44.4% named child care or home-making as the main reason.[2]

  3. Women Are Paid Less When They Work

    Women in regular salaried jobs earned ₹18,353 a month on average in 2025, 75.8% of men's ₹24,217. In cities the figures were ₹21,653 and ₹27,973.[2]

  4. Women Live Longer After 60

    A woman who reaches 60 now lives another 19.8 years on average, and a man 17.5. Both have risen since 2007-11.[3]

  5. As Many Women Have an Account, Fewer Save in One

    In 2024, 89.2% of Indian women had a bank or mobile money account, as many as men, but 22.8% had saved money in one in the past year, against 31.1% of men.[4]

Put together: fewer women earn, they are paid less when they do, and they live longer, so money in their own name has more years to cover. Women who invest are using SIPs more. Their SIP assets grew from about ₹1,26,000 Crores in March 2021 to about ₹4,73,000 Crores in March 2026, from 15.2% to 29.8% of their mutual fund assets.[1]

How a Woman Can Start Investing, Step by Step

  1. Invest in your own name. To invest you need a PAN, KYC (proof of identity and address, recorded once with a KYC registration agency) and a bank account in your own name.[8]
  2. Name your nominees. From 1 Sep 2026 you can name up to 3 nominees on a mutual fund folio, and a new folio in one name needs a nomination or a signed choice not to nominate.[9]
  3. Know your number. At the calculator's numbers, the corpus needed at 60 is ₹6,17,18,783. After what your savings so far could grow to, the gap is ₹6,17,18,783, and a SIP of ₹17,485 a month fills it at the rate you chose: an illustration, not a promise of any return.
  4. Plan for a long life. A woman who reaches 60 lives another 19.8 years on average, and half live longer.[3] Planning 5 more years, to 90, needs a corpus of ₹7,24,17,257.
  5. Start early: waiting costs. Starting 5 years later, at 35, needs ₹24,304 a month, about 1.4 times as much.
  6. Keep going through a break. If you step away from paid work for a while, you can lower a SIP, or stop it and start a new one later; what is already invested stays invested. The Step-Up SIP Calculator shows what raising it afterwards does.
  7. Keep an emergency fund in your name. In 2024, 22.6% of Indian women said they could raise emergency money within 30 days without great difficulty, against 37.4% of men.[4] An emergency fund of a few months' expenses means a bad month need not end a SIP.

Questions Women Ask About Investing

Can a housewife or homemaker invest in mutual funds?

Yes. Anyone with a PAN, KYC and a bank account in her own name can invest, whether or not she earns. If your spouse gives you money to invest, the income it earns is usually added to your spouse's income for tax. The money itself is not taxed when you receive it, because a spouse counts as a relative.[10] Based on income-tax rules in force on 29 Sep 2026. This is general information, not tax advice. Please consult a tax professional.

Can I start a SIP if I have no income of my own?

Yes. A SIP needs KYC and a bank account in your name, not an income, and many schemes accept small monthly amounts: the Low Minimum SIP screen lists them. Money your spouse gives you is yours to invest, though the income it earns is usually taxed as your spouse's.

Is there a special mutual fund scheme for women?

No. SEBI's mutual fund rules set no different terms for women investors.[7] Every scheme open to an individual is open to a woman on the same terms, so the choice rests on your goal and how long until you need the money, as it does for anyone.

Are the tax rules different for women?

No. No tax rate, slab or deduction in the Income-tax Act, 2025 depends on whether you are a woman or a man.[10] Based on income-tax rules in force on 29 Sep 2026. This is general information, not tax advice. Please consult a tax professional.

Who pays tax on money my spouse gives me to invest?

If your spouse gives you money to invest, the income it earns is usually added to your spouse's income for tax. The money itself is not taxed when you receive it, because a spouse counts as a relative.[10] Based on income-tax rules in force on 29 Sep 2026. This is general information, not tax advice. Please consult a tax professional.

Should a woman invest differently from a man?

Not because she is a woman. What differs from person to person is goals, income, time and the years after work. Women live longer on average, so a retirement corpus may need to last longer: change the calculator's last age to see the difference.

Do you earn anything extra when a woman invests for the first time?

Yes, and we say so because distributors must disclose what they earn. SEBI requires fund houses to pay a distributor a one-time extra commission for each woman who invests in mutual funds for the first time (a new PAN). It is 1% of her first lump sum, up to ₹2,000, if she stays invested for a year; or 1% of what her SIP puts in during its first year, up to ₹2,000. It comes from money each fund house must set aside for investor education, and is not paid on some schemes, such as ETFs and overnight and liquid funds.[7] It does not reduce the amount you invest.
