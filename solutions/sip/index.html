SIP Investment: How to Start and Keep Going

How much should your SIP be, and will you keep it going when markets fall?

89 per 100

From April to August 2026, for every 100 new SIPs registered, 89 SIPs were closed or reached the end of their term.

Source: AMFI SIP data, April to August 2026. Worked out by us: 2.58 crore SIPs closed or completed ÷ 2.88 crore new SIPs × 100 = 89.4.[1]

A SIP does its work only while it keeps running. Almost as many SIPs close as start, and a SIP stopped early gives up its later years, when compounding adds the most.

SIP Stoppage Ratio: How Many SIPs Stop Early

  1. SIP Money Has Grown 8 Times

    Money paid in through SIPs rose from ₹43,921 Crores in 2016-17 to ₹3,49,589 Crores in 2025-26, about 8 times. In August 2026 alone it was ₹32,297 Crores.[1]

  2. Almost as Many SIPs Close as Start

    For every 100 new SIPs, 52 were closed or ran their term in 2023-24, 76 in 2024-25 and 89 from April to August 2026. A closed SIP may have finished its chosen term, failed its instalments, or been stopped by the investor.[1]

  3. What Stopping After 3 Years Costs

    At an assumed 12% a year, ₹3,000 a month kept for 15 years illustrates ₹15,13,728. Stopped after 3 years and left invested, the same SIP illustrates ₹5,46,971 at year 15. The gap is 12 years of instalments and their growth.[1]

  4. Most Households Hold No Mutual Fund

    63% of Indian households know of at least one securities market product, such as mutual funds or shares. Only 9.5% invest in one, and 6.7% hold mutual funds or ETFs.[2]

Starting a SIP is the easy part; keeping it going is harder. In March 2026, 34% of SIP money in regular plans had stayed invested for more than 5 years, and 19% for less than a year. Part of the gap reflects how fast new SIPs have grown.[3]

How to Start a SIP and Keep It Running

  1. Start with an amount you can keep paying. At the calculator's numbers, ₹3,000 a month for 15 years puts in ₹5,40,000 and illustrates ₹15,13,728 at the rate you chose: an illustration, not a promise of any return.
  2. Work back from your goal. If you know what you are saving for, the Goal SIP Calculator works out the monthly amount it needs once prices have risen.
  3. Start now. A SIP started later with the same end date has fewer instalments, and it loses the earliest ones, which grow the longest. The Cost of Delay Calculator shows what a delay costs.
  4. Keep it going: the later years add the most. Keeping the same SIP for 5 more years, 20 in all, illustrates ₹29,97,444, about 2.0 times as much.
  5. Keep going when markets fall. A fixed monthly amount buys more units when prices are low and fewer when they are high, so a SIP stopped in a fall buys nothing at the lower prices. Its value can stay below what you have paid in for months or years: in equity funds, invest through a SIP only money you will not need for at least 5 years.
  6. Keep the money in your bank on the debit date. Under SEBI's guidelines, a monthly SIP is treated as ceased once 3 instalments in a row fail (2 in a row for quarterly and other longer intervals).[1] If money is short for a while, ask us whether the SIP can be paused instead.
  7. Raise it as your income rises. The Step-Up SIP Calculator shows what raising the SIP every year does.
  8. Keep an emergency fund beside it. With a few months of expenses set aside, a sudden bill need not stop the SIP or force a sale in a falling market. The Emergency Fund page works out how much.

SIP Questions People Ask

What happens if I stop a SIP?

Only the new instalments stop. The units you have bought stay invested, and you can redeem them when you choose. In the worked example, ₹3,000 a month stopped after 3 years and left invested illustrates ₹5,46,971 at year 15, against ₹15,13,728 if it keeps going, at an assumed 12% a year.

Can I pause a SIP instead of stopping it?

Some fund houses let you pause a SIP for a few months and restart it without a new form; whether a scheme allows it, and for how long, is the fund house's choice. A pause still means fewer instalments, so ask us before you stop or pause.

What happens if a SIP instalment fails?

Under SEBI's guidelines, a monthly SIP is treated as ceased once 3 instalments in a row fail (2 in a row for quarterly and other longer intervals).[1] The units already bought stay invested. Your bank may also charge for a failed debit.

Can a SIP make a loss?

Yes. A SIP buys units at market prices, and their value can fall below what you have paid in, most of all in equity schemes and over short periods. A SIP spreads your buying over time; it does not protect against losses.

Can I stop a SIP and withdraw my money at any time?

You can stop a SIP at any time, and redeem the units of an open-ended scheme on any business day, apart from units still in an ELSS lock-in. An exit load may apply if you redeem soon after investing, as each scheme's documents set out. Under SEBI's rules, redemption money must reach your bank account within 3 working days, or the fund house pays interest at 15% a year for the delay.[4]

Is a SIP a plan, a fund or a way of investing?

A way of investing. A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) is an instruction to invest a fixed amount in a mutual fund scheme at regular intervals, usually every month. The scheme is the fund; the SIP is how the money goes in, and the same scheme also takes one-time investments.

Is money from a SIP taxed?

Each SIP instalment buys units with their own date, so each is taxed on its own when redeemed. In an equity-oriented fund, gains on units held up to 12 months are taxed at 20%; after that, at 12.5% on the part above ₹1,25,000 a year. In a debt fund, gains on units bought from 1 Apr 2023 are taxed at your slab rate.[5] Based on income-tax rules in force on 29 Sep 2026. This is general information, not tax advice. Please consult a tax professional.

What is the SIP stoppage ratio?

The number of SIPs closed in a period for every 100 new SIPs registered in it. From April to August 2026 it was 89 per 100.[1] The closed SIPs include those that finished their chosen term, those treated as ceased after failed instalments and those investors stopped, and they are not the same SIPs that started.
