How to Start Investing: Know Yourself First

Where should you start, given your goals, your debts and your nerve?

74%

Of Indian mutual fund and ETF investors, 74% prefer low risk, and only 9% are comfortable with high risk.

Source: SEBI, Investor Survey 2025, p.64.[1]

Before you choose a fund, know which foundations are in place, how long your money can stay invested and what you would do in a fall. The self-check takes about 2 minutes, and your answers never leave this page.

Your Answers on Risk

Conservative. You would rather avoid falls than chase growth.

Moderate. You can accept some ups and downs for the chance of more growth, but large falls would worry you.

Aggressive. You can sit through large ups and downs, for years, for the chance of more growth.

This label only describes your answers above. It is not a risk profile or an assessment of suitability, and it does not point to any scheme or category. Before you invest, complete the risk profile on the investor platform, or speak to us.

How We Worked This Out

The checklist comes from questions 3 to 7, in this order: an emergency fund, cover, costly debt, then goals with a date. Health cover alone counts as in place while nobody depends on your income.

The label comes from questions 1, 2 and 8 to 10. Each answer scores 0, 1 or 2 points:

How Old Are You?
2 points: Under 25, 25 to 34. 1 point: 35 to 44, 45 to 59. 0 points: 60 or over.
How Steady Is Your Income?
2 points: A steady salary. 1 point: Business income. 0 points: Irregular, Retired.
What Have You Invested In Before?
2 points: Shares. 1 point: Mutual funds. 0 points: Nothing yet, Only FDs or RDs.
If an Investment Fell 20%, What Would You Do?
2 points: Invest more. 1 point: Wait. 0 points: Sell.
How Long Could You Leave Money Untouched?
2 points: 3 to 7 years, 7 years or more. 1 point: 1 to 3 years. 0 points: Under 1 year.

The points add up to between 0 and 10: 0 to 4 Conservative, 5 to 7 Moderate and 8 to 10 Aggressive. Answering Sell to “If an Investment Fell 20%, What Would You Do?” or Under 1 year to “How Long Could You Leave Money Untouched?” keeps the label Conservative whatever the total. Other questionnaires weigh answers in their own way, so they may give you another label.

Why Your Risk Appetite Matters

  1. Even People Who Invest Mostly Want Low Risk

    80% of Indian households prefer low risk. The share is 85% among people aged 61 and over, and still 71% among people who already invest in shares.[1]

  2. Not Everyone Links Higher Returns With Higher Risk

    Only 56% of people surveyed understood that investments offering high returns also carry high risk. In villages the share was 45%.[1]

  3. Long Term Means Different Things to Different People

    Asked what a long-term investment means, 60% of households said more than 7 years, 35% said 1 to 7 years and 4% said under a year. So the self-check asks about years, not words.[1]

  4. Most Learn About Investing From Friends and Influencers

    Of investors, 59% get their information from friends, family and colleagues and 56% from financial influencers on social media, and 62% make some of their decisions on influencers' advice. 21% use the websites of SEBI, AMFI and the exchanges.[1]

Most people want low risk, few link higher returns with higher risk, and many learn about investing from friends and influencers rather than from their own situation. Your own answers come first.

How to Start Investing in India, Step by Step

  1. Start with the foundations. Your answers put 2 of 4 foundations in place. Work through the checklist in its order before you choose a fund.
  2. Build an emergency fund first. Of Indian adults, 60.7% could keep paying their expenses for a month or less if their main income stopped.[2] A few months of expenses where you can reach them means a bad month need not end an investment. The Emergency Fund page works out yours.
  3. Cover the people who depend on you. Health cover for the family, and life cover for each earner with dependants. Insurance protects; it is not an investment for your goals.
  4. Clear costly debt. Paying off a credit card balance or a personal loan saves its interest for certain. A home loan is different: the EMI Management page works out both.
  5. Put a cost and a date on each goal. The years to a goal decide where its money can go; each goal page below shows the categories by time to the goal.
  6. Complete your KYC. To invest you need a PAN, KYC (proof of identity and address, recorded once with a KYC registration agency) and a bank account in your own name.[3]
  7. Start small, and keep going. A SIP lets you begin with a small monthly amount: the Low Minimum SIP screen lists the schemes that accept ₹100 or less. 40% of investors had made no fresh investment in the past year, most often because of poor performance.[1] Falls are part of investing in equity; a SIP keeps buying through them.
  8. Learn from official sources. The guides under Read and Share come from SEBI, NISM and NCFE, and SEBI's own guide shows how to check that anyone offering you investment advice is registered.
  9. Complete a full risk profile before you invest. The self-check is not one. The investor platform takes you through a risk profile when you open an account, and you can go through it with us.

Questions Beginners Ask

What is my risk profile?

A risk profile looks at your finances, your goals and how much loss you can bear, before you invest. This self-check is not one: the investor platform takes you through a risk profile when you open an account, and you can speak to us about it.

What are the types of risk profile?

There is no official list. Fund houses and distributors use their own questionnaires, often with three to five bands from conservative to aggressive. The label in the self-check uses three, and only describes your answers.

What is risk appetite?

How large a fall in value you are willing and able to sit through, for the chance of more growth. Willing is your nerve: what you would do after a 20% fall. Able is your situation: your age, how steady your income is and how long the money can stay invested.

Can I start investing with ₹1,000 or less?

Yes. Many schemes accept a SIP of ₹100 or less: the Low Minimum SIP screen lists them. Build your emergency fund first, and complete your KYC.

Should I build an emergency fund before investing?

Usually, yes. Of Indian adults, 60.7% could keep paying their expenses for a month or less if their main income stopped.[2] Without one, an emergency can force you to sell an investment at a bad time, or to borrow at a high rate.

Should I clear my loans before I invest?

Costly debt first: a credit card balance or a personal loan charges high interest, and clearing it saves that interest for certain. A home loan is different: its rate is lower and it runs for many years, so many people invest alongside it. The EMI Management page works out both.

What is the riskometer?

Every mutual fund scheme shows its risk on SEBI's riskometer, at one of six levels from Low to Very High. Fund houses review the level every month and tell the scheme's investors by email or SMS when it changes.[4] It describes the scheme, not you, so check that its level fits how much risk you can take.
