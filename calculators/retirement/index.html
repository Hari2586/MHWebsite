Retirement Calculator

This calculator estimates the corpus needed on the day you stop working to meet expenses that keep rising with inflation until a chosen age, and the monthly SIP that could build it. Both use steady rates you choose: one while saving and one, usually lower, after retirement.

Not a return, so the 2% to 13% limit does not apply. India's CPI inflation has mostly been between 4% and 7% a year in the last decade.

Asset Class Before Retirement

Assumed 12% a year. For reference, the mean 10-year rolling return of the Nifty 50, 1 Jun 2013 to 30 May 2023, was 12.93% (AMFI).

Asset Class After Retirement

Assumed 7% a year. For reference, the mean 10-year rolling return of the 10-year G-Sec, 1 Jun 2013 to 30 May 2023, was 7.20% (AMFI).

How the Retirement Calculator Works

First, today's expenses are raised by inflation to the retirement year. The corpus needed is what it takes to pay those expenses, rising with inflation every year, until the age you enter, while the rest stays invested at the after-retirement rate. That is the present value of a growing series of payments made at the start of each year:

Corpus = E × Σk=0N−1 [(1 + g) ÷ (1 + q)]k

where E is the first year's expenses, g inflation, q the after-retirement rate and N the years in retirement. The SIP then fills the gap between the corpus and what your savings could grow to, as in the Goal SIP calculator.

Worked Example

Age 30, stopping work at 60, planning for expenses to age 85. Monthly expenses of ₹50,000 today become ₹2,87,175 a month (₹34,46,095 a year) after 30 years of 6% inflation. Paying that, rising 6% a year, for 25 years from a corpus illustrated at 7% needs ₹7,71,48,478 at age 60. Building it over 30 years at 12% takes an illustrated SIP of ₹21,856 a month; the table shows the corpus reaching ₹0 in the year you turn 85.

Why Real Results Differ

  • Markets do not grow at a steady rate: returns change every year and can be negative, and the order of good and bad years changes the outcome. Poor years just before or after retirement weigh most.
  • Expenses rarely rise at one steady rate; health costs often rise faster.
  • Living longer than the age entered needs a larger corpus.
  • Tax on gains, exit loads and stamp duty reduce what you receive.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this a retirement plan?

No. It is an illustration of arithmetic at rates you choose, not a plan or advice. It leaves out pensions, EPF, rent and any other income.

Why use a lower rate after retirement?

Many people move to steadier assets once withdrawals start. The default is the Debt rate; you can pick another asset class.

Does the corpus run out exactly at the age I enter?

Yes, by design: the table shows the corpus paying every year's expenses and reaching about ₹0 in the final year, if every rate holds.
